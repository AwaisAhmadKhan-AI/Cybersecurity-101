# Digital Signatures and Certificates

## Why Do We Need Digital Signatures?

In the real world, we use handwritten signatures to:

- Agree to terms and conditions
- Authorize transactions
- Confirm identity
- Acknowledge receiving something

In the digital world, we cannot use a handwritten signature.

Instead, we use **digital signatures**.

---

# What is a Digital Signature?

A **digital signature** is a cryptographic method used to verify:

- **Authenticity** → Who created or sent the document.
- **Integrity** → The document has not been modified.

It uses **asymmetric cryptography** (public/private keys).

---

# How Digital Signatures Work

Suppose Bob wants to send a signed document to Alice.

### Step 1

Bob has:

- Original document
- Private key

---

### Step 2

Bob signs the document using **his private key**.

Only Bob has this key.

---

### Step 3

Bob sends:

- The original document
- The digital signature

to Alice.

---

### Step 4

Alice uses **Bob's public key** to verify the signature.

If verification succeeds:

- The document really came from Bob.
- The document hasn't been altered.

---

---

# Why Use the Private Key?

Only the owner possesses the private key.

Therefore:

- If the signature is valid,
- It proves the owner signed the document.

This provides **authentication**.

---

# Simple Form of Digital Signature

The simplest concept is:

```
Document

↓

Encrypt with Private Key

↓

Signature

↓

Receiver decrypts using Public Key

↓

Original document verified
```

---

# How Digital Signatures Work in Practice

Modern systems do **not** encrypt the entire document.

Instead:

1. Generate a **hash** of the document.
2. Encrypt the hash using the sender's private key.
3. Send:
   - Original document
   - Encrypted hash (digital signature)

The receiver:

1. Generates a hash of the received document.
2. Decrypts the sender's hash using the public key.
3. Compares both hashes.

If they match:

- The document has not changed.
- The sender is authentic.

---

# Electronic Signature vs Digital Signature

Some people use these terms interchangeably, but they are different.

## Electronic Signature

Example:

- Typing your name
- Drawing your signature
- Pasting an image of your signature

This **does NOT** prove:

- Integrity
- Authenticity

Anyone can copy and paste the image.

---

## Digital Signature

Uses:

- Private key
- Public key
- Cryptography

It proves:

- Authenticity
- Integrity

# Certificates

## What is a Certificate?

A **certificate** is a digital document that proves someone's identity.

It links:

- A person's or organization's identity
- Their public key

Certificates are widely used for **HTTPS** websites.

---

# Example

When you visit:

```text
https://tryhackme.com
```

How does your browser know it is the real website?

Because the website provides a **TLS certificate**.

---

# What Does a Certificate Do?

A certificate tells your browser:

> "This public key belongs to TryHackMe."

Your browser then verifies whether it should trust that certificate.

---

# Certificate Authority (CA)

A **Certificate Authority (CA)** is a trusted organization that issues certificates.

Examples include well-known commercial and public CAs.

Your:

- Operating System
- Browser
- Device

already trusts many root Certificate Authorities.

---

# Chain of Trust

Certificates work using a **chain of trust**.

```text
Website Certificate
        │
Signed By
        ▼
Intermediate CA
        │
Signed By
        ▼
Root CA
        │
Trusted By
        ▼
Browser / Operating System
```

If the browser trusts the Root CA, it also trusts certificates issued through that chain.

---

# HTTPS Certificate Verification

When visiting a secure website:

```text
Browser

↓

Receives Website Certificate

↓

Checks Certificate

↓

Verifies Chain of Trust

↓

Trusted?

↓

Yes

↓

Secure HTTPS Connection
```

---

# TLS Certificates

To enable HTTPS on a website, you need a **TLS certificate**.

You can obtain one:

- From a Certificate Authority (usually paid)
- From **Let's Encrypt** (free)

---

# Why HTTPS Matters

HTTPS protects data exchanged between:

- Browser
- Website

It provides:

- Encryption
- Authentication
- Integrity

---

# Key Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Digital Signature** | Cryptographic proof that verifies authenticity and integrity |
| **Private Key** | Used to create/sign a digital signature |
| **Public Key** | Used to verify a digital signature |
| **Certificate** | Digital document linking an identity to a public key |
| **Certificate Authority (CA)** | Trusted organization that issues certificates |
| **Root CA** | Top-level trusted Certificate Authority |
| **TLS Certificate** | Certificate used to secure HTTPS websites |
| **Chain of Trust** | The trust relationship from a website certificate back to a trusted Root CA |

---

# Summary

- Digital signatures verify **authenticity** and **integrity**.
- A digital signature is created using the **private key**.
- Anyone can verify it using the **public key**.
- Modern digital signatures usually sign a **hash** of the document instead of the entire document.
- Electronic signatures (like pasted images) are **not** cryptographic signatures.
- Certificates prove the identity of a website or organization.
- Certificate Authorities (CAs) issue trusted certificates.
- Browsers trust websites through a **chain of trust**.
- HTTPS relies on TLS certificates to provide secure communication.
